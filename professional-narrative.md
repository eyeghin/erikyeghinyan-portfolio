# My Financial Crime Career Narrative

## What I Have Actually Been Doing

My experience in financial crime did not begin with a textbook definition of AML. It developed across three different operating environments: digital customer onboarding, high-volume transaction monitoring, and frontline retail banking. Each role showed me a different point at which financial-crime risk enters a financial institution—and how important the handoff is between customer-facing teams, investigators, and compliance specialists.

## IDBank: Learning to Establish Who the Customer Is

At IDBank, my work centered on the beginning of the customer relationship. I reviewed identity information, documents, onboarding data, and account context to determine whether information was complete, consistent, and reasonably supported.

The work required more than checking whether fields were filled in. I compared identity details, looked for inconsistencies or suspicious documents, used KYC, CIP, CDD, and EDD principles, reviewed source-of-funds context when relevant, and used OSINT to better understand risk. When information did not align, I documented the issue, sought clarification through appropriate customer outreach, and escalated cases that needed enhanced review.

This role taught me a foundational lesson: transaction monitoring is only as strong as the customer profile behind it. If expected activity, identity, occupation, or account purpose is unclear, it becomes much harder to distinguish legitimate behavior from activity that requires investigation.

## Guidehouse and Green Dot Bank: Following the Activity

At Guidehouse, supporting Green Dot Bank, I moved deeper into the investigative lifecycle. I worked in a high-volume environment and completed more than 30 transaction-monitoring investigations per day using Unit21.

The alerts involved activity such as ACH transfers, P2P payments, unusual spending, suspected account takeover, and fraud indicators. My responsibility was not to repeat the alert rule. I had to determine what the activity meant in the context of the customer.

I reviewed the alert trigger, transaction history, timing, velocity, values, channels, counterparties, prior behavior, and available KYC information. I used OSINT and adverse-media research when relevant, separated facts from assumptions, and looked for both risk indicators and reasonable explanations. I then documented the investigation in a way that another reviewer could follow: what occurred, what I reviewed, what the evidence showed, and why the case should be closed or escalated.

When activity remained unexplained or presented material risk, I escalated it and supported SAR narrative development. When the evidence reasonably explained the activity, I documented the basis for closure. Both decisions required judgment. Escalating everything would not be effective risk management, and closing activity without adequate analysis could expose the institution to risk.

By improving how I organized evidence and wrote conclusions, I increased investigation efficiency by 25% and helped reduce false-positive escalations by 30%, while maintaining production, quality, AML/BSA, FinCEN, sanctions, OFAC, and audit expectations.

## Santander Bank: Seeing Risk at the First Line

At Santander Bank, I experienced financial-crime controls from the customer-facing side as a Teller and Relationship Banker. Frontline banking is a first line of defense: it is often where identity concerns, unusual cash activity, transaction context, possible scams, and changes in customer behavior first become visible.

I verified identity, applied KYC and CIP procedures, processed deposits, withdrawals, transfers, payments, and account maintenance, and followed cash-control, privacy, regulatory, and operational requirements. I listened carefully to the customer's explanation, gathered relevant context, recognized fraud or AML red flags, and escalated concerns to the appropriate teams rather than attempting to perform a second-line investigation from the branch.

This role strengthened my understanding that good compliance and good customer service are not opposites. Asking a clear question, protecting a customer from a potential scam, handling sensitive information carefully, and escalating with urgency can protect both the customer and the bank.

## How the Three Roles Connect

| Stage | My experience | Risk contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Customer entry | IDBank onboarding and compliance operations | Establish identity, expected activity, and initial risk |
| Activity review | Guidehouse / Green Dot transaction monitoring | Analyze behavior, follow funds, document decisions, and escalate |
| Customer interaction | Santander frontline banking | Recognize red flags, gather context, apply controls, and refer concerns |

Together, these roles gave me an end-to-end understanding of financial-crime risk. I understand how customer data is established, how activity is monitored, how an investigator reaches a defensible decision, and how frontline observations can strengthen an investigation.

## The Value I Bring

I bring investigative discipline, operational speed, and communication skills. My Communication degree helps me translate complex transactional activity into writing that is concise, objective, and easy to review. My experience working across Armenia, Lithuania, and the United States—and communicating in Armenian, English, and Russian—helps me approach customers and evidence with cultural awareness while remaining focused on policy and facts.

I am most effective in roles where I can examine patterns, ask why activity differs from expectations, connect information across systems, and produce a clear decision that protects the institution without losing sight of the legitimate customer.
